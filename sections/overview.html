Overview

Understanding how self-awareness emerges has been a central question in cognitive science for decades. Through a combination of controlled behavioral experiments and computational theories, researchers have investigated how humans identify themselves, discover their own agency, and construct internal representations of their bodies. Inspired by these foundations, we introduce two complementary paradigms for evaluating self-centric intelligence in embodied MLLMs: Experiment Translation, which transfers cognitive experiments into embodied environments, and Theory Translation, which operationalizes cognitive theories as evaluation frameworks. Together, these frameworks assess multiple dimensions of self-centric intelligence, ranging from primitive sensorimotor agency and body-schema formation to explicit self-recognition.

Cognitive Science Background

Contingency Detection Theory (Watson, 1972; Gergely & Watson, 1999)
A foundational account of early agency development proposing that the sense of self emerges from detecting reliable contingencies between one's actions and subsequent environmental changes. The theory emphasizes discovering consistent action–effect regularities and identifying which events are under control.

Evaluation Framework

Minimal Agency Discovery
We translate contingency-based theories of early agency into an abstract sensorimotor evaluation paradigm. By reducing the problem to pure action–effect contingencies, it evaluates whether an agent can discover which entity is under its own control and acquire a primitive sense of agency through sensorimotor interaction.

Cognitive Science Background

Comparator Model Theory (Wolpert et al., 1995; Frith et al., 2000)
A prominent account of the sense of agency positing that self-generated actions are identified by comparing predicted and observed outcomes. Agency arises from attributing causality to oneself in the presence of multiple potential sources of action and environmental disturbances.

Evaluation Framework

Self–Other Causal Attribution
We extend agency evaluation by introducing competitive multi-agent environments. The framework features heterogeneous agents, adversarial interactions, and event-driven feedbacks. MLLMs must infer which outcomes are attributable to themselves and distinguish self-generated consequences from externally induced events.

Cognitive Science Background

Body Schema Theory (Held & Hein, 1958; Gallagher, 2006)
A central theory of embodied cognition proposing that the brain maintains an internal model of the body's structure and movement capabilities to support coordinated action. The body schema provides a persistent representation of what constitutes one's body and how it can be controlled.

Evaluation Framework

Embodied Self-Representation
We instantiate body-schema theory in photorealistic 3D environments featuring human and robotic embodiments. By requiring agents to infer hidden action semantics and maintain coherent control across perspective transformations, it evaluates whether embodied MLLMs can construct stable internal representations of their bodies and action capabilities.

Cognitive Science Background

Mirror Self-Recognition Test (Gallup, 1970; Rochat, 2003)
A classic paradigm in comparative psychology and developmental science. In the mark test, a visible mark is secretly placed on an individual's body; successful recognition is demonstrated when the individual uses a mirror to inspect or touch the mark on itself rather than on the reflected image.

Evaluation Framework

MirrorBench
We extend the classical Mirror Self-Recognition paradigm to embodied physical simulation, enabling the evaluation of self-centric intelligence in MLLMs. The benchmark features a four-level protocol spanning mirror perception, mirror reasoning, mirror discovery, and explicit self-recognition.
